Hence, new control strategies can be designed to regulate both demand and supply of Low-Altitude Air city Transport (LAAT) systems. This can be achieved by adjusting aircraft departure times, and manipulating transfer aircraft flows at boundary air regions. In this research, new modelbased control strategies are designed, where aircraft departure management and boundary control strategies are integrated. The aviation operation can benefit from the proposed floworiented control paradigm, which can balance the LAAT system’s supply and demand , i.e. controlling the transfer flow between airspace regions and simultaneously managing the aircraft departure (inflow). The current paper presents the development of different control strategies: Departure Controller (DC), Boundary Controller (BC), and integrated Departure and Boundary Controller (DBC), with supporting simulation results. The designed controllers are tested in a new LAAT framework that considers modeling and control of LAAT operation while capturing the microscopic and macroscopic levels simultaneously.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, the development of a new mode of urban air transport, the low-altitude passenger and delivery aircraft, emerges from the advancement of aviation and communication technologies. This advances the concept of urban air mobility (UAM), leading to the invention of new urban air transport systems, namely low-altitude air city transport (LAAT). The literature highlights the growing interest in deploying low-altitude aircraft in the airspace ( Doole et al. , 2020 ; Bulusu et al. , 2021 ). Countless aircraft designs are currently in development, and most aircraft are electrically powered, utilize advanced batteries, and are capable of vertical takeoff and landing (VTOL) behavior. The VTOL aircraft have the capabilities to fly (vertically or horizontally) and hover depending on the flight mission. These relatively small aircraft can be remotely or autonomously controlled (operated with or without pilots) and may be multi-rotors, fixed-wing, or tilt-wing. This paper collectively refers to these aircraft as low-altitude aircraft. LAAT systems will include the low-altitude aircraft transferring passengers and goods in urban areas using low-altitude levels of urban airspace. LAAT operations can utilize urban transport networks by including a third-dimensional space for additional mobility alternatives. However, several new operational issues and questions are being raised, see Bulusu (2019 ), Straubinger et al. (2020 ), Bauranov and Rakas (2021 ) and Cohen et al. (2021 ). Given the high complexity of individual movements of aircraft in the 3D space, traffic control strategies can be designed with a hierarchical control approach, to control flows of aircraft in specific regions within the network by utilizing the concept of Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (MFD). While there are some similarities between urban ground traffic systems and LAAT systems, there are also additional challenges that require revisiting both the network modeling and control based on MFD dynamics. The operation of LAAT traffic in the era of automation and communication differs greatly from that of ground traffic. The key flow-oriented differences between ground traffic and air traffic at the aggregation level of individual entity (vehicle or aircraft) are divided into two main challenges: modeling and control. In modeling, the direction and dimensionality are extended, i.e. the aircraft have multi-directional flows traveling in the 3rd dimension. In control, the aircraft interactions are more complex, especially in collision avoidance, while the safety constraints are very restrictive. In addition, all LAAT aircraft can be fully controlled, as an unmanned or manned aircraft must follow the given commands (to change route or velocity) by the control center. Traffic urban management of LAAT traffic can coordinate and integrate their controlled subsystems, as they are expected to be based on digital technology and telecommunications, see Straubinger et al. (2020 ). Advanced technologies are enabling the futuristic aviation ecosystem. Currently, expert humans are responsible for most safety critical tasks, which represent the lowest maturity levels. In the future, aircraft operations and airspace management are expected to be essentially autonomous, which represent the highest maturity levels with high density airspace. The UAM Maturity Level (UML) was recently presented in Goodrich and Theodore (2021 ) which is aligned with this work perspective that advanced traffic control strategies can be relied on the upcoming technology. Connectivity and digitalization will enable new control measures in aviation operations and open new ways for integrating these measures in real time urban traffic management. Hence, new control strategies can be designed to regulate LAAT demand and supply. This can be achieved by manipulating aircraft departures, aircraft routings, aircraft speed, etc. In this paper, new model-based control strategies are designed, where aircraft departure management and boundary control are integrated , as depicted in Fig. 1. While the urban ground systems are still limited in controllability, the aviation operation can benefit from the proposed flow-oriented control paradigm, which can balance the LAAT system’s supply anddemand . It should be noted that LAAT controlled systems might create system queues, which have a high impact on the system’s efficiency and can lead to environmental and economical challenges. Therefore, it is worth investigating the interaction and synchronization of controlling both the input (demand) and transfer traffic flows (supply) of LAAT systems. The investigation focuses on balancing the constraints and capabilities of a LAAT system to achieve system stability. To the authors’ knowledge, integrated departure management andboundary control strategies for LAAT systems have never been investigated. Although the proposed boundary control in this paper for LAAT systems has the same concept of the perimeter control in urban ground traffic, i.e. metering the flows transferring from one region to another, yet they are implemented differently due to the different dynamics of vehicles and aircraft (such as queuing vs. hovering, traveling in two-spatial-dimensional flow vs. three-spatial-dimensional flow, etc.) and the different structural configurations of the networks (2D vs. 3D). In the case of perimeter control in ground systems, vehicles can be metered by traffic signals and wait to transfer to the next region by queuing on roads at the perimeter. On the other hand, in the case of the boundary control in LAAT systems, in this paper we design the boundary control while leveraging the 3rd spatial dimension. Controlled aircraft are directed to move to a buffer zone above the region (within the same layer) and wait there by hovering, as illustrated in the enlarged right box in Fig. 1. While waiting in the buffer zone, located far from the boundary, can improve the operational performance of the boundary, it may introduce other issues that could potentially degrade the traffic performance, such as an increase in the number of conflicts between aircraft and higher energy (battery) consumption. It should be noted that aggregate boundary queue dynamics have been investigated in Haddad (2017b) or Ni and Cassidy (2020) for urban ground systems, yet introducing them for LAAT systems imposes new dynamics and control issues that should be addressed both at the microscopic behavior (e.g., collision avoidance) and at the macroscopic level in terms of controlling the flows entering and exiting the buffer (or exiting a region and entering a region). Traffic flow models are needed to develop integrated strategies for LAAT systems. Following the operational concept of controlling flows of aircraft traffic, a modeling approach from the traffic flow theory can be adopted. The literature shows that the Macroscopic Fundamental Diagram (MFD) is a powerful modeling approach for understanding and managing vehicular networks from a theoretical as well as an application-oriented perspective. The MFD aggregates traffic information from the whole network and states traffic variables that inform us of the network condition (free flow or congestion). MFD as a concept was first proposed in Godfrey (1969), revisited by Mahmassani et al. (1987) and Daganzo (2007), while it was experimentally proven to exist for urban areas in Geroliminis and Daganzo (2008). The interested readers can refer to Haddad (2017b), Ni and Cassidy (2020), Sirmatel et al. (2021), Johari et al. (2021) and Menelaou et al. (2022) for previous MFD works. In terms of the dynamics representation of the MFD-based model for a single region, one can refer to Small and Chu (2003) and Daganzo (2007) and for multi-region systems to Ramezani et al. (2015) and many other studies. The advantages of utilizing an MFD-based model to solve the LAAT control problem is that the complexity is reduced by aggregating the information and control actions at the macroscopic level (networklevel), and simultaneously transforming the control actions to the microscopic level (vehicle-level). Such hierarchical modeling and control approach is preferable compared to a global control approach, where only the microscopic level is being controlled directly. Compared to a global control approach, hierarchical modeling and control approaches in large-scale traffic networks allow for a reduction in the number of states and control inputs in the control problem, and increase the space for a feasible set of optimization problems. We believe that it is theoretically possible to develop collective and aggregate aircraft traffic flow models for LAAT systems, and based on that, to design feedback traffic control strategies. There has been very limited exploration of using traffic flow models for LAAT systems in the literature. The studies in Battista and Ni (2017), Ivan Tereshchenko and Zou (2020), Cummings and Mahmassani (2021, 2022), Aarts et al. (2023), Hoogendoorn et al. (2023) and Cummings and Mahmassani (2024) focused on the macroscopic level for LAAT systems, and showed a similar behavior of MFD shape to ground traffic, however, only the free flow conditions are presented. In Haddad et al. (2021), a congested regime is shown in the MFD curve while considering the virtual queues that surround the airspace. While in Safadi et al. (2023a), different case study examples were simulated to analyze the full shape of MFD curves for LAAT systems, considering different algorithms, aircraft, and airspace settings, where a congested regime is sufficiently presented. Recently, an adaptive boundary control strategy considering the aggregated behavior of aircraft flows in large-scale LAAT systems was developed in Haddad et al. (2021). The strategy was designed to operate a LAAT network at a critical or desired accumulation state, generated from a reference model. The control strategy manages the transfer of aircraft, i.e. the output traffic LAAT flow, at the boundaries of the urban regions. It should be stressed that in Haddad et al. (2021) only the boundary control was considered; and the control-oriented model and the plant model were the same developed aggregated MFD-based model, i.e. the lower level complexity of collision avoidance and individual aircraft interaction were not considered. Finally, although an aircraft departure management for LAAT system was designed in Safadi et al. (2023b), only the departure control was examined with a simplified MFD-based model, which does not include the transfer flows between regions within the network. This paper investigates multi-regional model-based traffic management for LAAT systems. Feedback control strategies are developed based on a new accumulation-based model as a means to manage the input andtransfer traffic flows in the airspace. Hence, (i) a new aggregate control-oriented LAAT model is formulated in this paper, serving as a control model for designing the integrated departure and boundary control strategy. The new dynamics model the aggregated behavior of aircraft flows in LAAT operation, while utilizing advanced control strategies, and capture the system queuing behavior; (ii) an original feedback control strategy is designed in this paper, which integrates departure management and boundary control methods. The real-time strategy is designed to minimize the system delays, including departure and boundary delays. The control and plant models are different, i.e. the control model is based on macroscopic aggregated behavior, while the plant model is based on microscopic individual aircraft behavior. The developed feedback control strategies at the macroscopic level are transformed to be implemented at the microscopic level using an aircraft command subsystem and its algorithms, in an attempt to verify the optimality of the airspace conditions. ## 2. Developing LAAT aircraft departures and boundary flows model with MFD-based dynamics for integrated control design

### 2.1. LAAT model-based operational framework

A new LAAT framework that couples modeling and control of LAAT systems is developed, as shown in Fig. 2. The developed framework integrates the two aggregation levels, i.e. microscopic and macroscopic. In the presented framework, the microscopic model is utilized as a plant model, which describes the ‘‘reality’’ with detailed aircraft behavior, while the macroscopic model is utilized as a control model to design the control inputs. The traffic LAAT dynamics of the microscopic level steer the aggregate dynamics of the macroscopic level, which its characteristics variables are inserted as an input to the control model. Simultaneously, a feedback control strategy is designed to operationally optimize the airspace state following the airspace dynamics and identification. Then, the calculated optimal control inputs are transferred and transformed to the microscopic level via an aircraft command subsystem that optimizes the aircraft departure times and controls the aircraft at the regional boundary. The presented framework allows us to investigate different control strategies while utilizing airspace dynamics. The goal is to study three strategies: (i) departure management, i.e. departure time optimization, (ii) boundary control, i.e. controlling the aircraft prior to flying through a virtual boundary; and (iii) integrated departure and boundary control.

A collision avoidance algorithm should control the aircraft to avoid collisions with other aircraft or obstacles. To control LAAT aircraft, two types of space around each aircraft Aare defined: (i) safety space 𝑆A, and (ii) avoidance space 𝐴A. The safety space𝑆Ais determined to avoid a conflict and the avoidance space 𝐴Ais determined to start avoidance control. The aircraft avoids a collision with a predefined safety radius 𝑟s,Aand avoidance radius 𝑟a,A, by determining the aircraft control input 𝐯c,Ausing an Artificial Potential Field (APF) approach based on Lyapunov-Like functions. In addition, following a decentralized control approach, a detection space for each aircraft is defined. The detection space DAof aircraft Ais centered in its position 𝐩A(𝑡)with the detection radius𝑟d,A. The aircraft’s objective is to follow predefined waypoints and avoid collision with moving obstacles. The aircraft model has two properties: (i) conflict-free, i.e. even if an aircraft enters into the safety space of another aircraft, it can keep away from the neighboring aircraft rapidly; and (ii) convergence to desired waypoints is guaranteed. Moreover, regarding practicability, the developed control has three features: (i) a practical motion model is used, i.e. a double integrator model with a velocity command as an input is proposed for aircraft; (ii) detection of neighboring aircraft IDs of an aircraft is not required by the control; and (iii) control saturation is imposed, i.e. the maximum velocity command in the controller is restricted according to a priori determined requirement.

### 2.3. Macroscopic fundamental diagram for LAAT networks

By aggregating the aircraft trajectories in the network, it is possible to identify the macroscopic level variables to capture the airspace traffic conditions and to construct the MFD. We adopt the spatial structure that divides the airspace into several vertical latitudes and each latitude into different regions. The MFD concept can be determined for the whole network or per region, where the boundaries of each region need to be determined to evaluate the macroscopic traffic flow variables. Furthermore, as shown in

The main traffic characteristics and physics laws of vehicular traffic flow theory can be generalized to aircraft traffic flow by adapting the one-spatial-dimensional flow to three-spatial-dimensional flow. We follow the generalized definitions of Edie (1963) to calculate the MFD variables. Using the aircraft trajectories, the travel time and distance of each aircraft for a fixed time period are measured and aggregated, to calculate the main macroscopic traffic flow variables, i.e. flow 𝑄(𝑡), density𝐾(𝑡), and speed 𝑉(𝑡). In addition, other macroscopic traffic flow variables are calculated such as accumulation 𝑛(𝑡), outflow𝐺(𝑡), and production 𝑃(𝑡), which are evaluated according to the regions and network boundaries and the position of aircraft. These macroscopic traffic flow variables enable the development of an accumulation-based model. Moreover, to determine the airspace performance, additional factors are estimated such as: queues and delays. It is common in the literature on traffic systems, e.g. Papageorgiou et al. (2003) and Haddad (2017a), to compare the total time spent in the system when investigating different strategies which can be also used here to reflect accurately the LAAT system performance by aggregating travel time and waiting time of aircraft. By estimating the above macroscopic traffic flow variables, one can identify macroscopically the airspace state, i.e. if the airspace is in free flow or congestion conditions. This estimation allows to model the airspace dynamics and then to develop a control model for the design of a feedback control strategy. The development of the control model is described in Section 2.4.

### 2.4. Departures and boundary flows model

Following the introduced LAAT model-based operational framework, a macroscopic model allows us to capture the airspace conditions and to model the aggregated behavior of aircraft flows in LAAT network. The proposed model, namely departures and boundary flows model , can manipulate the departure of aircraft (inflow rate) and control the transfer flows between neighboring regions. The model can be utilized as a control model to apply traffic control strategies, in order to optimize the airspace conditions and operate LAAT system efficiently. ### 2.5. Traffic control strategies

The optimal control problem (4)-(8) and (13)-(23) is solved by applying a Model Predictive Control (MPC) approach, see e.g. Geroliminis et al. (2013). MPC is a form of rolling horizon control in which the current control inputs are obtained by solving a finite-horizon open-loop optimal control problem at each time step, with a current state feedback from the plant being the initial state of the model. The open-loop optimization problem yields a sequence of optimal control inputs after several iterations of solving nonlinear programming, and the first control action in this sequence is applied to the plant, then the procedure is carried out again. This scheme of feedback control, i.e. the feedback loop of states from the plant to the model as initial states for the optimization, can handle errors between the prediction model and the plant. The MPC controller obtains the optimal control sequence for the prediction horizon 𝐍pby solving an optimization problem formulated with the prediction accumulation-based model, where 𝛥k[s]is the time step of the MPC controller. ### 3.1.1. Airspace setting

The airspace setting defines the flight’s area and rules, similar to ground traffic networks where the roads and nodes (intersections or roundabouts) are defined. Airspace setting components include the geometry of the area, flight routes, and airspace structure. In this work, we follow the airspace structure presented in Haddad et al. (2021 ).A 3D network , which allows flights in three dimensional space X-Y-Z, is investigated. Within the network, there is no predetermined structure of flight routes, allowing aircraft to fly in any direction. The network has a size of 1.5 [km] by1.5 [km] by120 [m] and is divided into two regions, the city center (region 1) and the periphery (region 2) in each layer. Fig. 3 shows this structure. Each aircraft’s origin and destination are randomly (uniformly) set inside the ground-level network ( 𝑧= 0). The aircraft are designed to model VTOL behavior, meaning they enter and exit the network with vertical takeoff and landing.

## 4. Conclusions and future research directions

The main contribution of this paper is developing a new aggregate control-oriented LAAT model which is realized in a novel LAAT framework that (i) models the LAAT traffic flow, and (ii) implements advanced model-based traffic control strategies. The control strategies are actuated through suggested command algorithms to demonstrate the new integrated departure and boundary control method. This paper develops traffic control strategies for LAAT systems by utilizing the MFD concept. An original framework that captures the microscopic and macroscopic levels of LAAT operation was developed. The traffic control strategies are applied at the microscopic and macroscopic levels. The results demonstrate that the total time spent in the system can be decreased by implementing integrated departure and boundary control strategy for different traffic conditions, which is aligned with the different UAM Maturity level density scale. Note that this paper proposes an integrated strategy of departure and boundary control that efficiently balances the demand and supply of LAAT systems. The results suggest that controlling active aircraft has a more significant impact on enhancing the system’s efficiency than controlling aircraft waiting to depart. The optimization problem’s weight calibration indicates that minimizing boundary queues is crucial compared to departure queues. This is because aircraft at boundary queues are still active while hovering, which consume more energy. Current research, Safadi et al.
